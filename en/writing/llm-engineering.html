How LLM application engineering evolves: from prompt to loop, and what comes next

July 2026 · Reynolds Liu

Each new concept solves the bottleneck the previous layer hit at scale

ConceptBottleneck it solvesLevel
Prompt engineeringhow to phrase a single instructiontoken
Context engineeringwhat goes in the window, and what gets cutwindow
Harness engineeringwhat the model can touch — tools, environment, permissionsenvironment
Loop engineeringhow it iterates, self-corrects, and knows when to stoptime

The general rule for how a new discipline gets named: when a sub-step's failure modes, required skills and tooling all diverge from the parent field, it splits off and takes a name of its own — the way context engineering was once just one step inside prompt engineering.

Extrapolating: the next disciplines to be named

  • Verification engineering — most likely to surface first. The trigger signal: verifying becomes harder than producing, because agent throughput far exceeds human review capacity. The core problems are designing task structures where verification costs far less than production, writing rubrics that resist reward hacking, judge models, and the statistics of sampled auditing.
  • Orchestration / fleet engineering. Multi-agent is an architecture; fleet is a discipline — the same relationship multithreading has to concurrent programming. Task decomposition, context-passing protocols between agents, conflict arbitration, failure detection and replacement, cost allocation — underneath, these are all old distributed-systems problems.

Discontinuities: scenarios that break the ladder

  • Economy engineering: central dispatch gives way to market mechanisms — bidding, bounties, compensation — and management becomes mechanism design.
  • Ecology engineering: you stop designing behaviour and start designing selection pressure. The engineer becomes a breeder rather than an architect.
  • De-engineering: the bet that all of this engineering is scaffolding for a period when models were not strong enough — the way deep learning evaporated feature engineering. What remains is intent specification: stating clearly what you want. Specification and requirements analysis, the oldest skills in the trade, come full circle to become the last moat.

Management work is becoming engineering artefacts

  • Task-assignment judgement → orchestrator routing logic (code you can A/B test)
  • Code review taste → a rubric file (version-controlled, iterable)
  • Onboarding docs → loadable agent skills (diffable, revertible, copyable ten thousand times)
  • Deciding to replace someone → an agent-swap policy triggered by eval scores

The difference: human management runs on oral tradition; engineering artefacts can be diffed, rolled back, tested and copied without limit.

Where to stand: bet on invariants, not tools

Invariants (appreciate over time)Tools (half-life in months)
Distributed systems, OS and database internalsTricks specific to one agent framework
Reading code and judging whether it is goodFramework specifics
Turning vague requirements into precise specifications
Statistical intuition — the foundation of eval and verification

Pick your position: stand upstream of agents — building orchestration, sandboxes and memory systems — or beside them, in the eval and reliability engineering that verifies their output. Do not stand downstream, on well-specified tickets, CRUD and bug fixes; that is the layer agents consume first. The way out is to shift from completing tasks to owning problems: carrying one system from requirement, through production, to the incident at 3am.

In one sentence

You do not need to predict which concept wins — only to stand where every scenario overlaps: deep systems fundamentals × fast judgement × the ability to say clearly what you want.
